Developing a Conceptual Framework

Abstract

Organisations increasingly invest in AI-powered mental health chatbots to address workplace mental health crises. While research shows that these tools can reduce depression and anxiety symptoms, much less is known about whether such improvements translate into work engagement outcomes. This conceptual paper develops a framework that links employees’ AI mental health chatbot usage to job engagement, defined as vigour, dedication, and absorption. Grounded in the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model, AI mental health chatbots are positioned as personal resources that may reduce psychological distress and thereby enhance engagement. The proposed framework incorporates psychological distress as a mediator and considers usage frequency, perceived usefulness, and organisational support as potential moderators. After reviewing evidence on the clinical effectiveness, workplace applications, and ethical challenges of AI mental health chatbots, the paper identifies a critical gap: existing research rarely examines job engagement directly, especially in high-pressure contexts such as Hong Kong. The paper concludes by outlining a survey-based research design for empirically testing the framework and discussing practical implications for organisations considering AI mental health chatbots as part of their well-being strategies.

1. Introduction

Workplaces around the world face a growing mental health crisis, with rising levels of stress, burnout, and psychological distress threatening organisational health and productivity (García-Madurga et al., 2024). In response, employers are exploring technology-enabled mental health solutions, including AI-powered chatbots that aim to deliver scalable, low-stigma psychological support. These tools promise 24/7 accessibility, perceived anonymity, and relatively low marginal costs compared with traditional counselling, making them attractive additions to employee well-being programmes (Li et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2025).

The Hong Kong context is especially relevant. Hong Kong employees are known for long working hours, with union surveys suggesting that more than half of workers log over 45 hours per week and a significant minority exceed 70 hours (South China Morning Post, 2023). Corporate reports indicate that many workers experience high levels of stress, burnout, and concentration difficulties, with poor “mind health” emerging as a major concern (AXA, 2024). At the same time, stigma surrounding mental health and concerns about confidentiality may discourage employees from seeking traditional face-to-face counselling, particularly when support is provided through their employer. In this environment, digital tools such as mental health apps and AI-enabled chatbots are increasingly marketed as accessible, bilingual, and less stigmatising options for support. User-centred development of web-based mental health platforms, including chatbot-style interfaces, has been shown to support uptake and engagement when design aligns with users’ needs (Mak et al., 2023), offering a relevant precedent for local adoption.

Clinical and quasi-clinical research provides some evidence that AI conversational agents can reduce depression and distress symptoms. For example, Li et al. (2023) report moderate reductions in depression and distress scores across multiple randomised controlled trials of AI-based conversational agents. However, this body of work focuses primarily on clinical outcomes and general well-being, rather than on work-related attitudes and behaviours. Reviews of AI applications in workplace well-being highlight the growth of AI-based monitoring, coaching, and support tools, but rarely establish clear links between these interventions and employee job engagement (García-Madurga et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025). This creates a critical evidence gap for decision-makers who invest in AI mental health chatbots in the hope of improving both well-being and engagement.

This paper addresses that gap by developing a conceptual framework that connects AI mental health chatbot usage, psychological distress, and employee job engagement. Drawing on the JD–R model, the framework conceptualises AI mental health chatbots as personal resources that can help employees manage psychological demands. Psychological distress is proposed as a key mediator, while usage frequency, perceived usefulness, and organisational support are considered as moderators that may strengthen or weaken the pathway from chatbot usage to engagement. The focus is on employees in organisations that have access to AI mental health chatbots as part of formal well-being initiatives, with particular attention to Hong Kong as a high-pressure, service-oriented economy.

Research objectives and questions.

The study pursues three main objectives:

  • To investigate the relationship between AI mental health chatbot usage and employee job engagement.
  • To examine whether psychological distress mediates the relationship between chatbot usage and job engagement.
  • To explore whether usage frequency, perceived usefulness, and organisational support moderate these relationships.

Corresponding research questions include:

  • What is the relationship between AI mental health chatbot usage and job engagement (vigour, dedication, absorption)?
  • Does psychological distress mediate the relationship between chatbot usage and job engagement?
  • Do usage frequency, perceived usefulness, and organisational support moderate the effectiveness of chatbots in enhancing engagement?

2. Construct Definitions

Clear construct definitions are essential for developing a coherent and testable framework. This section defines the focal constructs used in the proposed model and links them to commonly used measures where appropriate.

AI mental health chatbot usage.

AI mental health chatbots are software applications that use natural language processing and machine learning to conduct therapeutic or supportive conversations, delivering interventions such as cognitive behavioural therapy, mindfulness exercises, and psychoeducational content (Li et al., 2023; Ni & Jia, 2025). In workplace settings, these chatbots may be embedded within employee assistance programmes or wellness platforms. For this framework, chatbot usage refers to employees’ interaction with such tools, captured along several dimensions: access (whether an employee has used the chatbot), frequency (how often it is used over a given period), duration (time spent per session), and the types of features engaged (e.g., mood tracking, coping exercises, psychoeducation).

Psychological distress.

Psychological distress is defined as a state of emotional suffering that may include depressive symptoms (e.g., hopelessness, loss of interest), anxiety symptoms (e.g., excessive worry, nervousness), and non-specific complaints such as irritability or sleep disturbances (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). In workplace contexts, distress often precedes burnout and disengagement. Empirically, distress is commonly measured using validated self-report scales such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) for depression and the Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) for anxiety (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006), including in recent chatbot trials (Xu & Ma, 2025). Lower scores on these scales indicate reduced distress.

Job engagement.

Job engagement is conceptualised as a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind characterised by vigour, dedication, and absorption (Schaufeli et al., 2006). Vigour refers to high levels of energy, mental resilience, and willingness to invest effort in one’s work; dedication captures a sense of significance, enthusiasm, and challenge; and absorption reflects being fully concentrated and happily engrossed in work tasks. Engagement is typically measured using the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES), which includes items for each of the three dimensions (Schaufeli et al., 2006).

Moderators and contextual factors.

Several factors may influence the extent to which AI mental health chatbots improve job engagement. Usage frequency may determine whether meaningful changes in distress are achieved; perceived usefulness reflects the extent to which employees believe the chatbot helps them cope or improve their well-being; and organisational support captures signals from management and HR that mental health is valued and that chatbot usage is safe and encouraged (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017; García-Madurga et al., 2024). Additional contextual factors, such as privacy concerns, trust in AI, and local cultural norms around help-seeking, are particularly salient in Hong Kong, where hierarchical workplace cultures and high job insecurity may shape how comfortable employees feel using employer-linked mental health tools (AXA, 2024; Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025).

3. Critical Literature Review

3.1 Clinical Effectiveness of AI Mental Health Chatbots

A growing body of research evaluates AI conversational agents for mental health support. Li et al. (2023) synthesise evidence from 35 studies, including 15 randomised controlled trials, and report moderate reductions in depression (Hedges’ g ≈ 0.64) and distress (Hedges’ g ≈ 0.70) for users of AI-based conversational agents compared with controls or baseline measures. These effects are strongest for multimodal, generative AI-based agents integrated with mobile messaging, suggesting that continuous, convenient access enhances engagement and outcomes. A scoping review of AI chatbots for health professionals’ psychological health reported beneficial effects on anxiety, burnout, and depression in several studies, while noting variability in design and the need for standardised protocols (Baek et al., 2025). A randomised trial comparing CBT-based chatbots with high versus low social cues found that designs richer in social cues led to greater reductions in depression and anxiety (measured by PHQ-9 and GAD-7) and to higher adherence, satisfaction, and therapeutic alliance (Xu & Ma, 2025).

The effectiveness of chatbots appears to depend on relational and design factors rather than technological sophistication alone. Li et al. (2023) highlight the importance of human–AI therapeutic relationships, content engagement, and effective communication for user satisfaction and clinical improvement. Other reviews map AI interventions across screening, support, monitoring, prevention, and education, noting that AI tools are most commonly used for support and self-management rather than as standalone treatments (Ni & Jia, 2025).

Despite encouraging evidence on symptom reduction, these studies rarely address whether improvements translate into functional outcomes that matter to organisations, such as work performance or engagement. Moreover, general psychological well-being measures sometimes show weaker or inconsistent improvements, raising questions about the scope of benefits (Li et al., 2023). This gap is particularly problematic when organisations adopt chatbots with the explicit expectation that better mental health will lead to higher engagement.

3.2 Workplace Applications and Adoption Challenges

Within workplace settings, AI mental health chatbots are typically introduced as part of broader well-being programmes or digital benefits. Real-world programmes have demonstrated feasibility and uptake in large workforces, with chatbots used for triage and navigation to live support (Jackson-Triche et al., 2023). A systematic review of AI in workplace well-being identifies applications in stress monitoring, emotional support, and personalised interventions, but notes that many interventions are still at pilot or case-study stages (García-Madurga et al., 2024). Yuan et al. (2025) document more than 50 mental health-related chatbots, including 22 large language model–based tools, and argue that while these systems can provide accessible support, they often deliver generic content that does not fully address job-specific stressors such as workload pressures, role ambiguity, or interpersonal conflict.

Some workplace-focused studies offer promising but partial evidence. Banerjee et al. (2024) describe how chatbot-enhanced Mental Health First Aid can be integrated into corporate wellness frameworks, potentially reducing stigma and improving early detection. West et al. (2023) show that behavioural science–informed AI interventions can substantially increase employee assistance programme utilisation. However, these studies largely focus on engagement with mental health services (e.g., EAP access rates) rather than engagement with work tasks. The distinction is important: employees may become more engaged with support resources without necessarily becoming more energetic, dedicated, or absorbed in their jobs.

Ethical and practical challenges further complicate workplace adoption. Qualitative work on bots for mental health in organisations emphasises that human and technology agency interact—leadership, HR practices, and organisational context shape how chatbot insights are acted upon and thus how much they affect wellbeing (Dutta & Mishra, 2023). A scoping review of conversational AI in mental health care identifies privacy and confidentiality as the most frequently discussed concerns, followed by safety, harm, and the opacity of algorithmic decision-making (Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025). Grabb et al. (2024) evaluate several state-of-the-art language models in mental health emergency scenarios and conclude that many models may cause harm or provide inadequate responses. In Hong Kong, where job insecurity and long hours are common, fears that mental health data might be used against employees could strongly discourage authentic chatbot use (AXA, 2024). These issues suggest that even clinically effective chatbots may fail to deliver organisational benefits if employees do not feel safe using them.

3.3 From Symptom Reduction to Job Engagement

The literature thus reveals a fundamental disconnect between what is known about AI mental health chatbots and what organisations need to know. Clinical research demonstrates symptom reduction, and workplace pilots show improved engagement with services, but the pathway from chatbot usage to job engagement remains under-explored (Li et al., 2023; West et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2025). This is problematic because many employers invest in AI mental health tools precisely to improve engagement and related performance outcomes.

Existing research only partially addresses the mechanisms that might link chatbot usage to engagement. Studies of AI adoption and mental health suggest that AI can both alleviate and exacerbate stress depending on implementation strategy and employee perceptions of control and fairness (Kim & Lee, 2024; Meduri et al., 2024). If AI tools are experienced as supportive, they may reduce distress and free up resources for work; if they are experienced as intrusive or unfair, they may increase stress and undermine trust. However, very few studies explicitly test whether employees who use AI mental health chatbots report higher vigour, dedication, or absorption at work.

Taken together, the evidence highlights an important research gap. Organisations are making investment decisions based on clinical evidence and usage metrics, but lack models that directly connect chatbot usage, psychological distress, and job engagement. The next section develops such a framework, using the JD–R model to specify the proposed relationships.

4. Conceptual Framework and Relationship Justification

The proposed framework is grounded in the JD–R model, which posits that job demands (e.g., workload, emotional strain) can lead to strain and burnout, whereas job and personal resources (e.g., social support, autonomy, coping tools) foster engagement (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). In this framework, AI mental health chatbots are conceptualised as personal resources that provide accessible coping strategies, emotional support, and psychoeducation. By helping employees manage psychological demands, chatbots may contribute to lower distress and higher engagement.

Direct relationship: Chatbot usage → Job engagement.

One possibility is that employees who actively use AI mental health chatbots feel more supported and better equipped to cope with stress, leading directly to higher engagement. For example, chatbot-guided breathing exercises or cognitive reframing techniques may help employees recover from daily hassles more quickly, enabling them to sustain energy and focus during demanding periods. In JD–R terms, chatbots provide a personal resource that can buffer the effects of job demands on engagement (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). This rationale motivates the first proposition:

  • P1: AI mental health chatbot usage is positively associated with employee job engagement.

Mediated relationship: Chatbot usage → Psychological distress → Job engagement.

A more nuanced view is that chatbot usage influences engagement primarily through its impact on psychological distress. If employees use chatbots to process negative emotions, challenge unhelpful thoughts, or access timely support, they may experience reduced distress (Li et al., 2023). In turn, lower distress can free cognitive and emotional resources, allowing employees to invest more fully in their work and experience higher vigour, dedication, and absorption (Schaufeli et al., 2006). This logic leads to the second proposition:

  • P2: Psychological distress mediates the relationship between AI mental health chatbot usage and job engagement.

Moderating factors.

The strength of these relationships is likely to depend on how employees use chatbots and how organisations implement and support them. Higher usage frequency may be necessary to achieve meaningful changes in distress; perceived usefulness may influence whether employees engage deeply with chatbot content; and organisational support may determine whether staff feel safe using chatbots for sensitive issues (García-Madurga et al., 2024; Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025). Poorly communicated or low-trust implementations may limit honest usage and reduce potential benefits. Accordingly, the framework proposes:

  • P3: The positive relationship between chatbot usage and reduced psychological distress is stronger at higher levels of usage frequency.
  • P4: The positive relationship between chatbot usage and reduced psychological distress is stronger when perceived usefulness is high.
  • P5: The positive indirect effect of chatbot usage on job engagement via psychological distress is stronger when organisational support for mental health and AI tools is high.

5. Methodological Considerations for Future Empirical Testing

Although this paper focuses on framework development, it also outlines how the proposed relationships could be tested empirically. A suitable approach would be a quantitative, cross-sectional survey targeting employees in organisations that have implemented AI mental health chatbots as part of their well-being strategy.

Sample and data collection.

The target sample would consist of employees from diverse sectors such as technology, healthcare, finance, and professional services, who have access to AI mental health chatbots through their employers. A sample size of around 400 respondents would provide adequate statistical power for structural equation modelling. Participants could be recruited via organisational email invitations that explain the study purpose and ensure confidentiality.

Measures.

Job engagement would be measured using the UWES (Schaufeli et al., 2006). Psychological distress could be assessed using the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scales (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006), which have been used in recent chatbot trials (Xu & Ma, 2025). Chatbot usage would be captured via self-report items measuring access, frequency, duration, and feature use. Perceived usefulness and organisational support could be measured using Likert-type items adapted from technology acceptance and organisational climate literature. Demographic and job-related variables (e.g., age, tenure, industry) would be collected as controls.

Analysis.

Data analysis would begin with descriptive statistics and correlation analysis, followed by structural equation modelling to test the proposed mediation and moderation effects (Hair et al., 2021). Mediation could be assessed using bootstrapped indirect effects, while moderation could be examined through interaction terms or multi-group comparisons. Model fit indices and robustness checks would be used to evaluate the adequacy of the proposed model.

Ethical considerations.

Given the sensitivity of mental health data, strict ethical safeguards are necessary. Participation should be voluntary, with informed consent and the option to withdraw at any time. Survey responses should be anonymised, and employers should not have access to individual-level data. Clear communication about data handling and the limits of AI mental health tools is essential to maintain trust and avoid harm (Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025; Grabb et al., 2024).

6. Discussion, Implications, and Limitations

This paper contributes to the emerging literature on AI mental health chatbots by explicitly linking chatbot usage, psychological distress, and employee job engagement. While existing research documents clinical benefits and increases in engagement with mental health services, the proposed framework focuses on engagement with work itself. By positioning AI mental health chatbots as personal resources within the JD–R model, the framework clarifies how symptom reduction may translate into attitudinal and behavioural outcomes that matter to organisations.

For practitioners, the framework suggests that investments in AI mental health chatbots should not be evaluated solely on clinical symptom scales or utilisation metrics. Instead, organisations should consider whether employees who use these tools report higher vigour, dedication, and absorption at work. The framework also highlights the importance of implementation quality: chatbots are more likely to deliver benefits when employees perceive them as useful, feel safe using them, and experience strong organisational support for mental health. In Hong Kong, where long working hours and high stress are common, combining technological solutions with a supportive culture may be particularly important.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the framework is conceptual and requires empirical testing. A cross-sectional survey design would limit causal inferences; future research could employ longitudinal or experimental designs to examine changes in distress and engagement over time. Second, self-report measures of distress, engagement, and chatbot usage are vulnerable to common-method bias and social desirability effects. Third, the effectiveness of specific chatbots may vary widely depending on design, content quality, and integration with human support, which cautions against generalising across tools.

Future research could extend this framework by exploring qualitative experiences of employees who use chatbots at work, comparing AI-based support with human-delivered interventions, or examining cross-cultural differences in perceptions of AI mental health tools. Such work would deepen understanding of the conditions under which AI mental health chatbots can meaningfully enhance both well-being and engagement in modern organisations.

7. Conclusion

This report adopted the Literature Review and Framework Development approach. It critically evaluated evidence on AI mental health chatbots in clinical and workplace settings and developed an improved conceptual model linking chatbot usage, psychological distress, and employee job engagement. The JD–R model provides a coherent theoretical base: chatbots are positioned as personal resources that may reduce distress and thereby support engagement, with mediation and moderation specified in five testable propositions. The identified gap—that existing research rarely examines job engagement directly, especially in high-pressure contexts such as Hong Kong—remains central. A survey-based design using the UWES, PHQ-9, GAD-7, and usage and organisational measures offers a practical path for future empirical testing. The framework is ready for refinement and validation in organisational settings.
