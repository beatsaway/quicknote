1) Title of the Study

How the Rise of AI Mental Support Chatbots Affects Employee Job Engagement

2) Objectives of the Study

  • To examine whether employees’ AI mental health chatbot usage is associated with higher job engagement, defined in terms of vigour, dedication, and absorption (Schaufeli et al., 2006).
  • To test whether psychological distress mediates the relationship between chatbot usage and job engagement, drawing on the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017).
  • To explore whether factors such as usage frequency, perceived usefulness, and organisational support moderate the effectiveness of AI mental health chatbots in enhancing employee engagement.

3) Literature Review

Workplaces face a growing mental health challenge, with rising levels of stress, burnout, and psychological distress undermining productivity and well-being (García-Madurga et al., 2024). In Hong Kong specifically, survey evidence suggests that employees work some of the longest hours in the world and report high rates of work-related stress and burnout (AXA, 2024; South China Morning Post, 2023). These conditions have prompted organisations to experiment with technology-enabled mental health solutions, including AI-powered chatbots that offer on-demand psychoeducation, coping exercises, and emotional support.

A growing body of research has examined AI mental health chatbots in clinical and quasi-clinical settings. A systematic review and meta-analysis of AI-based conversational agents found moderate effects on depression and distress symptoms, particularly when chatbots were integrated with mobile messaging and used consistently over time (Li et al., 2023). Reviews of large language model–based mental health chatbots similarly highlight rapid growth in available tools, but note that most systems provide generic emotional support rather than interventions tailored to specific workplace stressors (Yuan et al., 2025). In the workplace context, behavioural-science-based AI interventions have been shown to increase employee engagement with mental health resources—for example, raising employee assistance programme uptake from 5% to 22% (West et al., 2023)—yet these studies measure engagement with services rather than engagement with work.

The theoretical framing of this study is provided by the JD–R model, which argues that job and personal resources (such as social support or coping tools) help employees manage demands and foster engagement (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). AI mental health chatbots can be conceptualised as a form of personal resource that may reduce psychological distress and free up energy for work. Job engagement itself is defined as a positive, fulfilling work-related state of mind characterised by vigour, dedication, and absorption, and is commonly measured using the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (Schaufeli et al., 2006). Psychological distress in workplace research is often operationalised via brief validated instruments such as the PHQ-9 for depression and the GAD-7 for anxiety (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006).

At the same time, ethical and implementation challenges may limit the impact of chatbots in organisational settings. Scoping reviews highlight concerns about privacy, confidentiality, safety, and the “black box” nature of AI systems, which can reduce user trust and willingness to share sensitive information (Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025; Grabb et al., 2024). These issues are particularly salient at work, where employees may fear that mental health data could affect their career prospects. Overall, the literature suggests that AI mental health chatbots can reduce symptoms and increase engagement with support services, but there is little direct evidence on whether and how these tools improve employee job engagement, especially in high-pressure contexts such as Hong Kong. This gap motivates the present study.

  • Problems identified: Existing studies rarely examine job engagement outcomes directly, and many workplace chatbots are not tailored to job-specific stressors. Ethical concerns about privacy and data use are recurrent, particularly in employer-sponsored programmes.
