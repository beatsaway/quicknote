Abstract

Many organisations are starting to use AI mental health chatbots at work. Research suggests these tools can reduce depression and anxiety symptoms, but it is less clear if this also helps employees feel more engaged at work. This easy-English report reviews the literature, uses the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model to explain a simple pathway from chatbot use to psychological distress and then to job engagement, and notes Hong Kong issues such as long hours, stigma, and confidentiality worries. It explains managerial implications and gives clear recommendations, including using PHQ-9 and GAD-7 as common tools to help monitor depression and anxiety severity when organisations evaluate programmes.

1. Introduction

Mental health problems such as stress, burnout, and psychological distress are increasingly common in workplaces and can harm both employees and organisations (García-Madurga et al., 2024). In response, some employers are trying technology-based support, including AI chatbots, to provide low-stigma help that is available anytime (Li et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2025).

Hong Kong is a useful context for this topic. Many employees work long hours, and local reports suggest high stress and concentration problems (South China Morning Post, 2023; AXA, 2024). At the same time, stigma and confidentiality worries can stop employees from seeking face-to-face support, especially if support is linked to the employer. This makes private and easy-to-access tools (like apps and chatbots) more attractive (Mak et al., 2023).

Culture and workplace norms also matter. In more hierarchical workplaces, employees may worry that disclosing mental health issues could affect performance reviews, promotion, or job security. “Saving face” concerns can also make people less willing to talk openly about mental health. Because of this, trust and privacy safeguards are important.

Research shows AI mental health chatbots can reduce symptoms, but many studies focus on clinical outcomes, not on work outcomes. Reviews of workplace AI tools often discuss monitoring and support, but fewer studies directly link these tools to job engagement (García-Madurga et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025). This is a key gap, because organisations often hope that improved mental health will also improve engagement at work.

This report uses Approach 2: Literature Review & Recommendation. It reviews published studies, explains a simple JD–R-based pathway linking chatbot use, distress, and engagement, and ends with managerial implications and practical recommendations for organisations, with attention to Hong Kong.

Research objectives.

  • To explain how AI mental health chatbot usage may relate to employee job engagement.
  • To explain why psychological distress may sit in the middle of this relationship.
  • To give practical recommendations for organisations, including how to monitor mental health severity in a careful way.

2. Literature Review

2.1 Key concepts and measures

This section defines the main ideas so the review is easy to follow. This report does not run a survey.

AI mental health chatbot usage.

AI mental health chatbots use natural language processing and machine learning to hold supportive conversations and offer content such as CBT-style exercises, mindfulness, and psychoeducation (Li et al., 2023; Ni & Jia, 2025). In this report, “usage” means whether employees use the chatbot, how often they use it, and what features they use.

Psychological distress (PHQ-9 and GAD-7).

Psychological distress refers to emotional suffering, including depression symptoms (e.g., low mood, loss of interest) and anxiety symptoms (e.g., excessive worry) (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). Two common self-administered tools are PHQ-9 for depression severity and GAD-7 for anxiety severity, both based on symptoms over the past two weeks (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). This report does not administer these tools. Later sections recommend them only as practical options if organisations want to monitor mental health severity, with proper ethics and oversight.

Job engagement (UWES).

Job engagement is a positive work mindset with three parts: vigour (energy and resilience), dedication (meaning and enthusiasm), and absorption (being focused and fully involved) (Schaufeli et al., 2006). A common measurement tool is the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES) (Schaufeli et al., 2006).

Moderators and context.

The effects of chatbots may depend on how often employees use them, whether employees find them useful, and whether the organisation supports mental health and protects privacy (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017; García-Madurga et al., 2024).

2.2 Evidence that chatbots can reduce symptoms

A growing set of studies suggests AI mental health chatbots can reduce depression and distress symptoms (Li et al., 2023). Some studies also show that design matters. For example, chatbots with stronger “social cues” may lead to better outcomes and higher adherence, measured using PHQ-9 and GAD-7 (Xu & Ma, 2025). A scoping review for health professionals also reports benefits for anxiety, burnout, and depression in some studies, with a call for clearer protocols (Baek et al., 2025).

However, many studies focus on symptoms, not on work engagement. That matters for managers who want to know if better mental health also means better focus and energy at work (Li et al., 2023).

2.3 Workplace use and challenges

In workplaces, chatbots are often used as part of larger well-being programmes. Some programmes use chatbots to help triage and guide employees to live support (Jackson-Triche et al., 2023). Reviews also highlight important concerns about privacy, safety, and trust (Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025; Grabb et al., 2024). Workplace reviews note many pilots are still small-scale (García-Madurga et al., 2024), and some chatbots give generic advice that may not match real job stressors (Yuan et al., 2025).

2.4 The gap: symptoms vs job engagement

Even when symptoms reduce, we still need to explain why and how that might improve job engagement. Organisations often look at usage numbers, but usage does not automatically mean people are more engaged at work (Yuan et al., 2025). AI adoption can help or harm stress depending on how fair and supportive it feels (Kim & Lee, 2024; Meduri et al., 2024). This is why a clear pathway linking usage → distress → engagement is useful for managers.

2.5 Literature synthesis: a simple JD–R pathway

The JD–R model says that job demands can cause strain, but resources can support motivation and engagement (Demerouti et al., 2001; Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). In this report, AI mental health chatbots are treated as a personal resource. They may help employees cope, reduce distress, and support engagement.

AI chatbot usage
(access, frequency, features)
→
Psychological distress
(PHQ-9, GAD-7)
→
Job engagement
(UWES: vigour, dedication, absorption)
Potential moderators: usage frequency, perceived usefulness, organisational support (including privacy safeguards).

Simple expectations (for practice and future research).

Employees who use chatbots may feel more supported and may cope better with daily stress. This could help them stay focused and energetic at work (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017).

  • Expectation 1: AI mental health chatbot usage is positively associated with employee job engagement.

Chatbot usage may reduce distress by helping employees manage emotions and thoughts (Li et al., 2023). When distress is lower, employees may have more mental energy to engage with their work (Schaufeli et al., 2006).

  • Expectation 2: Psychological distress mediates the relationship between AI mental health chatbot usage and job engagement.

Moderators.

  • Expectation 3: The relationship between chatbot usage and reduced distress is stronger when usage frequency is higher.
  • Expectation 4: The relationship between chatbot usage and reduced distress is stronger when perceived usefulness is higher.
  • Expectation 5: The indirect effect of chatbot usage on engagement via distress is stronger when organisational support for mental health and AI tools is higher.

3. Discussion and Managerial Implications

Overall, the literature points to a clear story: chatbots may help symptoms, but organisations should not assume symptom relief automatically means higher job engagement. The JD–R pathway helps managers think about distress as a bridge between tool use and engagement.

Managerial implications.

Buying a chatbot is not the same as getting results. Managers and HR need trust, privacy, and real support from the organisation so employees feel safe to use the tool honestly.

In Hong Kong workplaces, employees may be cautious about openly talking about mental health. Hong Kong-specific evidence reports substantial perceived discrimination and concerns about workplace consequences (EOC, 2023). More generally, stigma can discourage people from seeking help (Corrigan, 2004). Because of this, methods that require open disclosure, such as interviews or focus groups, may face lower participation and higher social desirability bias in routine monitoring.

Finally, implementation quality matters. Chatbots are more likely to help when employees trust the tool, feel safe using it, and see clear privacy safeguards and organisational support.

3.1 Limitations

First, this is a literature review, so it does not prove cause and effect. Second, chatbots vary widely in quality and features, so results may differ across tools. Third, cultural explanations should be treated as reasonable ideas, but they still need careful evidence to confirm.

4. Recommendations

These recommendations are written for organisations that offer (or plan to offer) AI mental health chatbots at work.

  1. Use PHQ-9 and GAD-7 for monitoring (where appropriate). These are self-administered tools that can be completed privately and scored in a consistent way (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). This paper does not run a survey. Organisations should follow ethics rules and get professional advice if needed.
  2. Protect privacy and build trust. Explain what data are collected, who can see them, and how crises are handled. Link chatbot use to safe escalation paths (Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025; Grabb et al., 2024).
  3. Connect chatbots to real work stressors and human support. Do not rely on generic chatbots alone when workload, role clarity, or team conflict are the main problems (Yuan et al., 2025; Jackson-Triche et al., 2023).
  4. Measure more than clicks. Track whether distress goes down and, if possible, whether engagement improves—not only how many people signed up (Schaufeli et al., 2006).
  5. Design for Hong Kong context. Offer low-visibility access where possible, consider bilingual needs, and use leadership messages that support mental health without forced disclosure (Mak et al., 2023; AXA, 2024).

5. Conclusion

This report used Approach 2: Literature Review & Recommendation to explain how AI mental health chatbots may relate to job engagement. The JD–R model gives a simple logic: chatbots can act as a personal resource, reduce distress, and support engagement when usage and organisational conditions are right. The report also highlights the Hong Kong context, where stigma and confidentiality concerns can affect disclosure. For organisations that want to monitor mental health severity in a structured way, PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are recommended as practical tools, together with strong privacy practices and human support pathways—not chatbots alone.
