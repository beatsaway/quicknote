How the Rise of AI Mental Support Chatbot Affects Job Engagement

Abstract

Organisations increasingly invest in AI-powered mental health chatbots to address workplace mental health crises. While research shows chatbots effectively reduce depression and anxiety symptoms, little evidence demonstrates that these improvements translate into work engagement outcomes. This study investigates whether AI mental health chatbots can enhance employee job engagement.

Using a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design, data will be collected from 300-400 employees across diverse industries who have access to AI mental health chatbots in their organisations. The study will use measures including the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale, Patient Health Questionnaire-9, and Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7. Grounded in the Job Demands-Resources model, AI chatbots are positioned as personal resources that help employees manage psychological demands, which might enhance engagement. Structural equation modelling will test if psychological distress mediates the relationship between chatbot usage and engagement, while exploring moderating factors such as usage frequency, perceived usefulness, and organisational support.

1. Introduction

Workplaces face a growing mental health crisis with rising levels of stress, burnout, and psychological distress threatening organisational health and productivity. AI-powered mental health chatbots have emerged as scalable solutions offering immediate psychological support. Recent reviews document increases in AI applications for workplace well-being, including mental health monitoring, emotional support, and well-being programmes (García-Madurga et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2025). But AI implementation also raises concerns about surveillance, job insecurity, and fairness (Shaikh et al., 2025).

Clinical research demonstrates AI's effectiveness in detecting and monitoring stress-related issues, with large language models significantly improving stress detection capabilities (Liu et al., 2024). In workplace settings, AI technologies including chatbots and wearable devices can detect early signs of stress and burnout, enabling timely interventions (Jangid, 2024). Yet this validation exists primarily within clinical psychology, measuring symptom reduction rather than organisational outcomes. Evidence linking these improvements in wider context remains limited.

Reviews of large language model-based mental health chatbots identify over 50 mental health-related chatbots, including 22 LLM-based models targeting depression, anxiety, and stress (Yuan et al., 2025). Research examining AI adoption's mental health implications reveals that AI adoption impacts employee burnout through the mediating role of job stress (Kim & Lee, 2024). Similarly, human-centred AI for personalised workload management demonstrates that benefits depend substantially on implementation strategy, with effective integration requiring comprehensive training, high personalisation, and regular feedback (Meduri et al., 2024). Pilot studies demonstrate that behavioural science-based interventions delivered via AI agents significantly improve employee engagement with mental health resources, with 22% accessing employee assistance programmes compared to benchmark rates of 5% per year (West et al., 2023). Despite promising developments, challenges remain including data privacy, algorithmic bias, and ethical implications of continuous employee monitoring (Jangid, 2024).

This research proposal bridges the gap between clinical efficacy and organisational outcomes by investigating the unexplored pathway connecting AI mental health chatbots to employee engagement. Can AI mental health chatbots serve as a viable and effective intervention for enhancing employee job engagement?

1.1. Defining the Landscape: Core Constructs

Employee Job Engagement is a positive, fulfilling work-related state characterised by three dimensions: (1) Vigour: high energy, mental resilience, and persistence; (2) Dedication: significance, enthusiasm, and challenge derived from work; and (3) Absorption: full concentration and deep immersion in work tasks (Schaufeli et al., 2006).

AI Mental Health Chatbots: Software applications using natural language processing and machine learning to conduct therapeutic conversations, delivering evidence-based interventions including Cognitive Behavioural Therapy, mindfulness exercises, and psychoeducational content (Li et al., 2023; Ni and Jia, 2024). Key workplace value propositions include 24/7 accessibility, anonymity, and scalability.

Psychological Distress: A state of emotional suffering accompanied by depression symptoms (hopelessness, loss of interest), anxiety symptoms (excessive worry, nervousness), and non-specific symptoms (irritability, sleep disturbances) (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). In workplace contexts, this distress often precedes burnout and disengagement.

1.2. The Problem: A Disconnect Between Clinical Efficacy and Organisational Strategy

Corporate leaders increasingly invest in AI mental health chatbots to improve workforce engagement, operating on the assumption that supported employees are more engaged (García-Madurga et al., 2023). Yet existing research inadequately confirms this assumption. While evidence shows chatbots reduce anxiety scores on clinical scales (Li et al., 2023), little data demonstrates that this reduction leads to increased vigour, dedication, and absorption in daily work. This evidence gap leaves decision-makers without empirically-validated links between technology use and key performance indicators, making investments acts of faith rather than strategic decisions.

1.3. Research Problem

There is a critical disconnect between clinical evidence demonstrating AI chatbot effectiveness in reducing mental health symptoms (Li et al., 2023) and organisational evidence linking these interventions to employee engagement outcomes. The fundamental pathway connecting AI mental health chatbot usage to employee job engagement remains largely unexplored, with insufficient understanding of mediating mechanisms, particularly the role of psychological distress reduction (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). Consequently, organisational decision-makers lack empirical evidence to determine whether AI mental health chatbots effectively translate clinical improvements into work-related attitudes and behaviours such as vigour, dedication, and absorption (Schaufeli et al., 2006). This evidence gap prevents organisations from making informed strategic decisions about investing in AI-powered mental health support systems.

1.4. Research Questions

Primary Research Question: Can AI mental health chatbots serve as a viable and effective intervention for enhancing employee job engagement?

Secondary Research Question 1: What is the relationship between AI mental health chatbot usage and employee job engagement, as measured by vigour, dedication, and absorption?

Secondary Research Question 2: Does psychological distress mediate the relationship between AI mental health chatbot usage and employee job engagement?

Secondary Research Question 3: What are the key factors (e.g., frequency of use, perceived usefulness, organisational support) that moderate the effectiveness of AI mental health chatbots in enhancing employee engagement?

2. Literature Review

The integration of artificial intelligence in mental health care has emerged as a transformative force, yet the pathway from clinical symptom reduction to organisational outcomes remains largely uncharted. This review examines the evidence base for AI mental health chatbots, tracing their clinical effectiveness, workplace applications, and ethical challenges to identify critical gaps in understanding how these technologies influence employee engagement.

2.1. Clinical Effectiveness: Promise and Limitations

The clinical evidence for AI conversational agents presents a nuanced picture of effectiveness. Li et al. (2023) meta-analysis of 35 studies, including 15 randomized controlled trials, demonstrates substantial reductions in depression (Hedge's g 0.64) and distress (Hedge's g 0.7), particularly for multimodal, generative AI-based agents integrated with mobile messaging. Yet this same analysis reveals a critical limitation: no significant improvement in overall psychological well-being was found, suggesting these interventions effectively address specific symptoms but may have limited impact on broader well-being constructs. This symptom-specific effectiveness raises important questions about whether clinical improvements translate into functional outcomes that matter to organisations.

The mechanisms of effectiveness depend critically on relational factors rather than technological sophistication alone. Li et al. (2023) identify three factors shaping user experience: human-AI therapeutic relationships, content engagement, and effective communication. Successful implementation requires more than deploying advanced technology. It demands careful attention to the therapeutic alliance that develops between users and AI systems. This relational dimension becomes complex in workplace settings, where privacy concerns, trust, and perceived surveillance may fundamentally alter how employees engage with these technologies.

2.2. The Workplace Context: A Mismatch Between Technology and Need

When examining workplace applications, a striking disconnect emerges between the proliferation of mental health chatbots and their capabilities for addressing workplace needs. Yuan et al. (2025) comprehensive review identifies over 50 mental health-related chatbots, including 22 LLM-based models, yet reveals that these primarily provide generic emotional support rather than interventions tailored to workplace contexts. This is concerning given that workplace mental health challenges often stem from job-specific stressors like workload, role ambiguity, interpersonal conflicts, which generic chatbots may inadequately address.

Some researchers have begun addressing this gap. Banerjee et al. (2024) demonstrate how chatbot-enhanced Mental Health First Aid can be integrated into corporate wellness frameworks, offering accessible, stigma-free support for early detection and crisis intervention. Their work suggests potential for reducing workplace mental health stigma and improving cost-efficiency. Yet this research focuses on crisis intervention and early detection rather than the ongoing relationship between chatbot usage and sustained employee engagement. Similarly, West et al. (2023) show that behavioural science-based AI interventions can significantly improve employee engagement with mental health resources (22% access rates versus 5% benchmarks), but this measures engagement with services, not engagement with work tasks.

This distinction is crucial: research demonstrates that AI can increase engagement with mental health services, yet whether this translates into improved job engagement (vigour, dedication, and absorption at work) remains unanswered. The pathway from using a mental health chatbot to becoming more engaged at work is neither direct nor well-understood.

2.3. Ethical Challenges: Barriers to Workplace Adoption

The ethical landscape surrounding AI mental health chatbots reveals significant concerns that may impede workplace adoption. Rahsepar Meadi et al. (2025) scoping review of 101 articles identifies privacy and confidentiality as the most frequently discussed concern (61.4%), followed by safety and harm (51.5%). These concerns take on particular urgency in workplace settings, where employees may fear that disclosing mental health struggles could impact career advancement or job security. The "black box" nature of AI algorithms, discussed in 25.7% of articles, further complicates trust that employees cannot understand how their data is processed or how responses are generated, potentially undermining willingness to engage authentically with these systems.

Grabb et al. (2024) evaluation of ten state-of-the-art language models reveals that most could cause harm in mental health emergencies, failing to protect users and potentially exacerbating symptoms. Their finding that existing models are insufficient to match human professionals due to overly cautious or sycophantic responses highlights a fundamental limitation: current AI systems cannot navigate the nuanced, context-dependent nature of mental health support that human professionals provide. This limitation is especially problematic in workplace settings, where mental health challenges may be intertwined with job stress, organizational culture, and interpersonal dynamics that require sophisticated understanding.

These ethical and safety concerns create a paradox: organisations invest in AI mental health chatbots to support employees, yet the very technologies intended to help may raise privacy fears, fail in critical moments, or provide inadequate support. Successful implementation requires not only technological advancement but also careful attention to how these systems are integrated into organisational cultures and how employee concerns are addressed.

2.4. Design and Implementation: The Gap Between Principles and Practice

Research on chatbot design principles offers guidance, yet reveals challenges in translating principles into effective workplace implementations. Nieminen et al. integrative review identifies three core themes: explicit knowledge about design and domain, knowing your audience, and creating a safe space. These principles emphasize professional planning, target group definition, and addressing user needs. All are critical for workplace applications. Yet the review's focus on general mental health contexts raises questions about how these principles apply when the "audience" is employees who may have concerns about privacy, surveillance, and job security.

Technological limitations further complicate implementation. AlMakinah et al. (2025) assessment of advanced language models (GPT v4, Mistral Large, LLama V3.1) shows that while these models show promise in generating structured responses, they fall short in emotional depth and adaptability compared to human therapists. Trustworthiness, bias, and privacy challenges persist due to unreliable datasets and limited collaboration with mental health professionals. Their proposed federated learning framework addresses some concerns, but the fundamental question remains: can AI systems that lack emotional depth and adaptability effectively support employees in ways that improve work engagement?

This challenge becomes more pressing when considering the broader landscape of AI applications in mental health. Ni and Jia (2024) mapping of AI technologies across five phases of mental health care shows that these technologies are predominantly used for support, monitoring, and self-management rather than standalone treatments. While benefits include reduced wait times and improved symptom tracking, recurring challenges like algorithmic bias, data privacy risks, and workflow integration barriers highlight the need for ethical design and human oversight. In workplace settings, these challenges are compounded by the need to integrate chatbots into existing employee assistance programs, wellness initiatives, and organizational structures.

2.5. The Critical Gap: From Symptom Reduction to Work Engagement

The existing literature reveals a fundamental disconnect between what we know about AI mental health chatbots and what organisations need to know. While substantial evidence demonstrates clinical effectiveness in reducing depression and distress symptoms, the pathway from symptom reduction to work engagement remains unexplored. This gap is problematic because organisations invest in these technologies with the explicit goal of improving employee engagement, yet lack empirical evidence that this goal is achieved.

The workplace-specific research that exists offers promising directions but falls short of addressing the core question. Yuan et al. (2025) and Banerjee et al. (2024) demonstrate potential applications, but neither directly links chatbot usage to employee engagement metrics. West et al. (2023) show improved engagement with mental health resources, but this is engagement with services, not engagement with work. The critical mediating mechanisms like how reduced psychological distress translates into increased vigour, dedication, and absorption at work, remain unexamined.

This gap is compounded by insufficient understanding of moderating factors. Research suggests that implementation strategy matters (Meduri et al., 2024), and that behavioural science principles can enhance engagement (West et al., 2023), yet the specific features, implementation strategies, and contextual factors that maximize both clinical outcomes and organisational benefits require systematic investigation. The ethical frameworks and design principles established in general mental health contexts may not adequately address workplace-specific concerns about privacy, trust, and safety that influence employee willingness to engage.

Thus, the literature reveals a critical need: empirical research that directly examines the relationship between AI mental health chatbot usage and employee job engagement, investigates mediating mechanisms (particularly the role of psychological distress reduction), and explores moderating factors that influence effectiveness. Without such research, organisations are making investment decisions based on clinical evidence that may not translate to the organisational outcomes they seek.

3. Methodology

This study employs a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design. Data will be collected from 300-400 employees across diverse industries (technology, healthcare, finance, professional services) who have access to AI mental health chatbots in their organisations. Procedural remedies will minimise common method bias, including temporal separation of measures and clear instructions.

An online self-administered questionnaire will measure: (1) chatbot usage frequency and perceived usefulness, (2) employee job engagement using the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES) (Schaufeli et al., 2006), (3) psychological distress using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) (Kroenke et al., 2001) and Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) (Spitzer et al., 2006), and (4) demographic and organisational control variables. Grounded in the Job Demands-Resources model (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017), AI chatbots are positioned as personal resources that help manage psychological demands. Structural equation modelling will test whether psychological distress mediates the relationship between chatbot usage and engagement. Moderation analysis will examine how frequency of use, perceived usefulness, and organisational support moderate this relationship.
