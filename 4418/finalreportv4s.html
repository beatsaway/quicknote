A Simple Conceptual Framework

Abstract

Many organisations are starting to use AI mental health chatbots at work. Research suggests these tools can reduce depression and anxiety symptoms, but it is less clear if this also helps employees feel more engaged at work. This paper builds a clear framework linking chatbot use to job engagement (vigour, dedication, absorption). Using the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model, the framework treats chatbots as a personal resource that may reduce psychological distress and, as a result, support job engagement. Psychological distress is a mediator, and factors like usage frequency, perceived usefulness, and organisational support may strengthen or weaken the relationships. The paper also discusses Hong Kong, where long working hours, stigma, and confidentiality concerns may affect how people use these tools. This is not a survey study. Instead, the paper ends with practical recommendations, including suggesting PHQ-9 and GAD-7 as common tools to screen for and monitor depression and anxiety severity.

1. Introduction

Mental health problems such as stress, burnout, and psychological distress are increasingly common in workplaces and can harm both employees and organisations (García-Madurga et al., 2024). In response, some employers are trying technology-based support, including AI chatbots, to provide low-stigma help that is available anytime (Li et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2025).

Hong Kong is a useful context for this topic. Many employees work long hours, and local reports suggest high stress and concentration problems (South China Morning Post, 2023; AXA, 2024). At the same time, stigma and confidentiality worries can stop employees from seeking face-to-face support, especially if support is linked to the employer. This makes private and easy-to-access tools (like apps and chatbots) more attractive (Mak et al., 2023).

Culture and workplace norms also matter. In more hierarchical workplaces, employees may worry that disclosing mental health issues could affect performance reviews, promotion, or job security. “Saving face” concerns can also make people less willing to talk openly about mental health. Because of this, trust and privacy safeguards are important.

Research shows AI mental health chatbots can reduce symptoms, but many studies focus on clinical outcomes, not on work outcomes. Reviews of workplace AI tools often discuss monitoring and support, but fewer studies directly link these tools to job engagement (García-Madurga et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025). This is a key gap, because organisations often hope that improved mental health will also improve engagement at work.

This paper responds by building a conceptual framework linking AI chatbot use, psychological distress, and job engagement. The JD–R model is used as the main theory. The focus is on employees who have access to these chatbots through workplace well-being initiatives, with attention to Hong Kong as a high-pressure environment.

Research objectives.

  • To explain how AI mental health chatbot usage may relate to employee job engagement.
  • To explain why psychological distress may sit in the middle of this relationship.
  • To discuss factors that may make the relationship stronger or weaker (e.g., usage frequency, perceived usefulness, organisational support).

2. Key Definitions

This section defines the main ideas used in the framework so the argument is clear, even though this report does not conduct a survey.

AI mental health chatbot usage.

AI mental health chatbots use natural language processing and machine learning to hold supportive conversations and offer content such as CBT-style exercises, mindfulness, and psychoeducation (Li et al., 2023; Ni & Jia, 2025). In this report, “usage” means whether employees use the chatbot, how often they use it, and what features they use.

Psychological distress (PHQ-9 and GAD-7).

Psychological distress refers to emotional suffering, including depression symptoms (e.g., low mood, loss of interest) and anxiety symptoms (e.g., excessive worry) (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). Two common self-administered tools are PHQ-9 for depression severity and GAD-7 for anxiety severity, both based on symptoms over the past two weeks (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). This report does not administer these tools. It only suggests them as practical options if organisations want to monitor mental health severity.

Job engagement (UWES).

Job engagement is a positive work mindset with three parts: vigour (energy and resilience), dedication (meaning and enthusiasm), and absorption (being focused and fully involved) (Schaufeli et al., 2006). A common measurement tool is the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES) (Schaufeli et al., 2006).

Moderators and context.

The effects of chatbots may depend on how often employees use them, whether employees find them useful, and whether the organisation supports mental health and protects privacy (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017; García-Madurga et al., 2024).

3. Literature review (short summary)

3.1 Evidence that chatbots can reduce symptoms

A growing set of studies suggests AI mental health chatbots can reduce depression and distress symptoms (Li et al., 2023). Some studies also show that design matters. For example, chatbots with stronger “social cues” may lead to better outcomes and higher adherence, measured using PHQ-9 and GAD-7 (Xu & Ma, 2025).

3.2 Workplace use and challenges

In workplaces, chatbots are often used as part of larger well-being programmes. Some programmes use chatbots to help triage and guide employees to live support (Jackson-Triche et al., 2023). Reviews also highlight important concerns about privacy, safety, and trust (Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025; Grabb et al., 2024).

3.3 The gap: symptoms vs job engagement

Even when symptoms reduce, we still need to explain why and how that might improve job engagement. Organisations often look at usage numbers, but usage does not automatically mean people are more engaged at work (Yuan et al., 2025). This is why a framework linking usage → distress → engagement is useful.

4. Conceptual framework

The JD–R model says that job demands can cause strain, but resources can support motivation and engagement (Demerouti et al., 2001; Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). In this paper, AI mental health chatbots are treated as a personal resource. They may help employees cope, reduce distress, and support engagement.

AI chatbot usage
(access, frequency, features)
→
Psychological distress
(PHQ-9, GAD-7)
→
Job engagement
(UWES: vigour, dedication, absorption)
Potential moderators: usage frequency, perceived usefulness, organisational support (including privacy safeguards).

Proposed relationship 1: Usage → engagement.

Employees who use chatbots may feel more supported and may cope better with daily stress. This could help them stay focused and energetic at work (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017).

  • Proposed 1: AI mental health chatbot usage is positively associated with employee job engagement.

Proposed relationship 2: Usage → distress → engagement.

Chatbot usage may reduce distress by helping employees manage emotions and thoughts (Li et al., 2023). When distress is lower, employees may have more mental energy to engage with their work (Schaufeli et al., 2006).

  • Proposed 2: Psychological distress mediates the relationship between AI mental health chatbot usage and job engagement.

Moderators.

  • Proposed 3: The relationship between chatbot usage and reduced distress is stronger when usage frequency is higher.
  • Proposed 4: The relationship between chatbot usage and reduced distress is stronger when perceived usefulness is higher.
  • Proposed 5: The indirect effect of chatbot usage on engagement via distress is stronger when organisational support for mental health and AI tools is higher.

5. Discussion

Overall, the framework links chatbot usage, distress, and job engagement in a clear path. It helps explain why symptom reduction may matter for work outcomes, not just health outcomes.

5.1 Practical implications

Suggested monitoring approach (Hong Kong workplaces).

In Hong Kong workplaces, employees may be cautious about openly talking about mental health. Hong Kong-specific evidence reports substantial perceived discrimination and concerns about workplace consequences (EOC, 2023). More generally, stigma can discourage people from seeking help (Corrigan, 2004). Because of this, methods that require open disclosure, such as interviews or focus groups, may face lower participation and higher social desirability bias in routine monitoring.

For practical monitoring, this report suggests PHQ-9 and GAD-7 as self-administered tools that can be completed privately and scored consistently (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). Again, this paper does not run a survey. It only recommends these measures as options.

Finally, implementation quality matters. Chatbots are more likely to help when employees trust the tool, feel safe using it, and see clear privacy safeguards and organisational support.

5.2 Limitations

First, this is a conceptual framework, so it does not prove cause and effect. Second, chatbots vary widely in quality and features, so results may differ across tools. Third, cultural explanations should be treated as reasonable explanations, but they still need careful evidence to confirm.

6. Conclusion

This paper used a literature review and framework development approach to explain how AI mental health chatbots may affect job engagement. The JD–R model provides the main logic: chatbots can act as a personal resource, reduce distress, and support engagement. The paper also highlights the Hong Kong context, where stigma and confidentiality concerns can affect disclosure. For organisations that want to monitor mental health severity, PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are recommended as practical tools for screening and monitoring depression and anxiety severity.
