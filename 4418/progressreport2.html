1) Title of the Study

How the Rise of AI Mental Support Chatbots Affects Employee Job Engagement

2) Objectives of the Study

  • To examine whether employees’ AI mental health chatbot usage is associated with higher job engagement, defined in terms of vigour, dedication, and absorption (Schaufeli et al., 2006).
  • To test whether psychological distress mediates the relationship between chatbot usage and job engagement, drawing on the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017).
  • To explore whether factors such as usage frequency, perceived usefulness, and organisational support moderate the effectiveness of AI mental health chatbots in enhancing employee engagement.

3) Literature Review

Workplaces face a growing mental health challenge, with rising levels of stress, burnout, and psychological distress undermining productivity and well-being (García-Madurga et al., 2024). In Hong Kong specifically, survey evidence suggests that employees work some of the longest hours in the world and report high rates of work-related stress and burnout (AXA, 2024; South China Morning Post, 2023). These conditions have prompted organisations to experiment with technology-enabled mental health solutions, including AI-powered chatbots that offer on-demand psychoeducation, coping exercises, and emotional support. In Hong Kong, insurers, employee assistance providers, and some employers have begun to offer or trial digital mental health and chatbot-style support; local development of user-centred platforms such as stepped-care mental well-being tools illustrates this trend (Mak et al., 2023). Real-world programmes elsewhere have demonstrated feasibility and uptake in large workforces, with chatbots used for triage and navigation to live support (Jackson-Triche et al., 2023).

A growing body of research has examined AI mental health chatbots in clinical and quasi-clinical settings. A systematic review and meta-analysis of AI-based conversational agents found moderate effects on depression and distress symptoms, particularly when chatbots were integrated with mobile messaging and used consistently over time (Li et al., 2023). A scoping review of AI chatbots for health professionals’ psychological health reported beneficial effects on anxiety, burnout, and depression in several studies, while noting variability in design and the need for standardised protocols (Baek et al., 2025). A randomised trial of CBT-based chatbots with high versus low social cues found that designs richer in social cues led to greater reductions in depression and anxiety (measured by PHQ-9 and GAD-7) and to higher adherence, satisfaction, and therapeutic alliance (Xu & Ma, 2025). Reviews of large language model-based mental health chatbots similarly highlight rapid growth in available tools, but note that most systems provide generic emotional support rather than interventions tailored to specific workplace stressors (Yuan et al., 2025). In the workplace context, behavioural-science-based AI interventions have been shown to increase employee engagement with mental health resources—for example, raising employee assistance programme uptake from 5% to 22% (West et al., 2023)—yet these studies measure engagement with services rather than engagement with work.

The theoretical framing of this study is provided by the JD-R model, which argues that job and personal resources (such as social support or coping tools) help employees manage demands and foster engagement (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). AI mental health chatbots can be conceptualised as a form of personal resource that may reduce psychological distress and free up energy for work. Job engagement itself is defined as a positive, fulfilling work-related state of mind characterised by vigour, dedication, and absorption, and is commonly measured using the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (Schaufeli et al., 2006). Psychological distress in workplace research is often operationalised via brief validated instruments such as the PHQ-9 for depression and the GAD-7 for anxiety (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006), including in recent chatbot trials (Xu & Ma, 2025).

At the same time, ethical and implementation challenges may limit the impact of chatbots in organisational settings. Qualitative work on bots for mental health in organisations emphasises that human and technology agency interact—leadership, HR practices, and organisational context shape how chatbot insights are acted upon and thus how much they affect wellbeing (Dutta & Mishra, 2023). Scoping reviews highlight concerns about privacy, confidentiality, safety, and the “black box” nature of AI systems, which can reduce user trust and willingness to share sensitive information (Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025; Grabb et al., 2024). These issues are particularly salient at work, where employees may fear that mental health data could affect their career prospects. Overall, the literature suggests that AI mental health chatbots can reduce symptoms and increase engagement with support services, but there is little direct evidence on whether and how these tools improve employee job engagement, especially in high-pressure contexts such as Hong Kong. This gap motivates the present study.

A gap noted is the limited public reporting of which Hong Kong companies currently use AI mental support chatbots; the final report will strengthen this background where sources permit.
  • Conceptual model and construct links: Developed a mediated model with clearly linked constructs—chatbot usage → psychological distress (mediator) → job engagement—and moderators (usage frequency, perceived usefulness, organisational support). Each relationship is justified via the JD-R framework: chatbots as personal resources that may reduce distress and thereby support engagement (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017).
  • Measures and methodological note: Identified standardised measures for key constructs (Utrecht Work Engagement Scale, PHQ-9, GAD-7) and outlined how the framework could be tested empirically (e.g. cross-sectional survey with structural equation modelling) for inclusion in the final report. This project does not involve primary data collection.
  • Problems identified: Existing studies rarely examine job engagement outcomes directly, and many workplace chatbots are not tailored to job-specific stressors. Ethical concerns about privacy and data use are recurrent, particularly in employer-sponsored programmes.
  • Next steps: (a) Strengthen the Hong Kong background in the final report using local statistics and industry reports, including organisations and companies adopting or trialling AI mental support tools where reported (e.g., Mak et al., 2023); (b) refine and state hypotheses derived from the conceptual model in the final report; (c) complete the final report with full literature review, proposed framework with justified relationships, discussion and conclusion.
