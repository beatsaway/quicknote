Abstract

Organisations increasingly adopt AI mental health chatbots amid workplace mental health pressures. Evidence shows symptom reduction, but links to employee job engagement remain under-specified. This literature-based report synthesises clinical and workplace research, uses the Job Demands–Resources model to explain how chatbots may act as personal resources affecting psychological distress and engagement, and highlights Hong Kong contextual barriers such as stigma, confidentiality concerns, and long working hours. It discusses managerial implications for implementation, privacy, and evaluation, and recommends PHQ-9 and GAD-7 as self-administered tools for monitoring depression and anxiety severity alongside chatbot programmes.

1. Introduction

Workplaces around the world face a growing mental health crisis, with rising levels of stress, burnout, and psychological distress threatening organisational health and productivity (García-Madurga et al., 2024). In response, employers are exploring technology-enabled mental health solutions, including AI-powered chatbots that aim to deliver scalable, low-stigma psychological support. These tools promise 24/7 accessibility, perceived anonymity, and relatively low marginal costs compared with traditional counselling, making them attractive additions to employee well-being programmes (Li et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2025).

The Hong Kong context is especially relevant. Hong Kong employees are known for long working hours, with union surveys suggesting that more than half of workers log over 45 hours per week and a significant minority exceed 70 hours (South China Morning Post, 2023). Corporate reports indicate that many workers experience high levels of stress, burnout, and concentration difficulties, with poor “mind health” emerging as a major concern (AXA, 2024). At the same time, stigma surrounding mental health and concerns about confidentiality may discourage employees from seeking traditional face-to-face counselling, particularly when support is provided through their employer. In this environment, digital tools such as mental health apps and AI-enabled chatbots are increasingly marketed as accessible, bilingual, and less stigmatising options for support. User-centred development of web-based mental health platforms, including chatbot-style interfaces, has been shown to support uptake and engagement when design aligns with users’ needs (Mak et al., 2023), offering a relevant precedent for local adoption.

Cultural and contextual factors may also shape the relevance of AI chatbots in Hong Kong workplaces. In relatively high power-distance settings, employees may worry that disclosing distress could affect performance evaluations, promotion prospects, or job security. Norms around “saving face” and the stigma attached to mental health concerns can further increase reluctance to seek help through visible, employer-linked channels. These conditions can make anonymous, on-demand tools attractive, while simultaneously raising the importance of trust, privacy safeguards, and credible organisational support.

Clinical and quasi-clinical research provides some evidence that AI conversational agents can reduce depression and distress symptoms. For example, Li et al. (2023) report moderate reductions in depression and distress scores across multiple randomised controlled trials of AI-based conversational agents. However, this body of work focuses primarily on clinical outcomes and general well-being, rather than on work-related attitudes and behaviours. Reviews of AI applications in workplace well-being highlight the growth of AI-based monitoring, coaching, and support tools, but rarely establish clear links between these interventions and employee job engagement (García-Madurga et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025). This creates a critical evidence gap for decision-makers who invest in AI mental health chatbots in the hope of improving both well-being and engagement.

This report addresses that gap through a literature review and recommendation approach. It reviews evidence on AI mental health chatbots, synthesises a coherent explanation of how usage may relate to psychological distress and job engagement using the JD–R model, and draws out implications and actionable recommendations for managers and HR, with particular attention to Hong Kong as a high-pressure, service-oriented economy.

Research objectives.

The report pursues three objectives:

  • To examine the conceptual relationship between AI mental health chatbot usage and employee job engagement.
  • To examine whether psychological distress plausibly mediates the relationship between chatbot usage and job engagement.
  • To identify practical recommendations for organisations, including how mental health severity might be monitored responsibly.

2. Literature Review

2.1 Key concepts and measures

Clear definitions keep the review coherent. This report does not collect primary data; it relies on published evidence and established measurement tools referenced in the literature.

AI mental health chatbot usage.

AI mental health chatbots are software applications that use natural language processing and machine learning to conduct therapeutic or supportive conversations, delivering interventions such as cognitive behavioural therapy, mindfulness exercises, and psychoeducational content (Li et al., 2023; Ni & Jia, 2025). In workplace settings, these chatbots may be embedded within employee assistance programmes or wellness platforms. For this review, chatbot usage refers to employees’ interaction with such tools, captured along several dimensions: access (whether an employee has used the chatbot), frequency (how often it is used over a given period), duration (time spent per session), and the types of features engaged (e.g., mood tracking, coping exercises, psychoeducation).

Psychological distress (and why PHQ-9 and GAD-7 matter).

Psychological distress is defined as a state of emotional suffering that may include depressive symptoms (e.g., hopelessness, loss of interest), anxiety symptoms (e.g., excessive worry, nervousness), and non-specific complaints such as irritability or sleep disturbances (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). In workplace contexts, distress can precede burnout and disengagement. Importantly, “distress” should be more than a descriptive label: it should be linked to established measurement approaches. Two widely used self-administered tools are the PHQ-9, which screens for and monitors depression severity, and the GAD-7, which screens for and monitors generalised anxiety disorder severity, both referencing symptoms over the past two weeks (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). Because depression and anxiety are often comorbid, it is commonly recommended to consider both tools to capture the overall clinical picture. Later sections recommend how organisations might use PHQ-9 and GAD-7 when evaluating mental health initiatives; this report does not administer these questionnaires.

Job engagement.

Job engagement is conceptualised as a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind characterised by vigour, dedication, and absorption (Schaufeli et al., 2006). Vigour refers to high levels of energy, mental resilience, and willingness to invest effort in one’s work; dedication captures a sense of significance, enthusiasm, and challenge; and absorption reflects being fully concentrated and happily engrossed in work tasks. Engagement is typically measured using the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES) (Schaufeli et al., 2006).

Moderators and contextual factors.

Several factors may influence the extent to which AI mental health chatbots improve job engagement. Usage frequency may determine whether meaningful changes in distress are achieved; perceived usefulness reflects the extent to which employees believe the chatbot helps them cope or improve their well-being; and organisational support captures signals from management and HR that mental health is valued and that chatbot usage is safe and encouraged (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017; García-Madurga et al., 2024). Additional contextual factors, such as privacy concerns, trust in AI, and local cultural norms around help-seeking, may be particularly salient in Hong Kong, where hierarchical workplace cultures and job insecurity can shape whether employees feel comfortable using employer-linked mental health tools (AXA, 2024; Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025).

2.2 Clinical effectiveness of AI mental health chatbots

A growing body of research evaluates AI conversational agents for mental health support. Li et al. (2023) synthesise evidence from 35 studies, including 15 randomised controlled trials, and report moderate reductions in depression (Hedges’ g ≈ 0.64) and distress (Hedges’ g ≈ 0.70) for users of AI-based conversational agents compared with controls or baseline measures. These effects are strongest for multimodal, generative AI-based agents integrated with mobile messaging, suggesting that continuous, convenient access enhances engagement and outcomes. A scoping review of AI chatbots for health professionals’ psychological health reported beneficial effects on anxiety, burnout, and depression in several studies, while noting variability in design and the need for standardised protocols (Baek et al., 2025). A randomised trial comparing CBT-based chatbots with high versus low social cues found that designs richer in social cues led to greater reductions in depression and anxiety (measured by PHQ-9 and GAD-7) and to higher adherence, satisfaction, and therapeutic alliance (Xu & Ma, 2025).

The effectiveness of chatbots appears to depend on relational and design factors rather than technological sophistication alone. Li et al. (2023) highlight the importance of human–AI therapeutic relationships, content engagement, and effective communication for user satisfaction and clinical improvement. Other reviews map AI interventions across screening, support, monitoring, prevention, and education, noting that AI tools are most commonly used for support and self-management rather than as standalone treatments (Ni & Jia, 2025).

Despite encouraging evidence on symptom reduction, these studies rarely address whether improvements translate into functional outcomes that matter to organisations, such as work performance or engagement. Moreover, general psychological well-being measures sometimes show weaker or inconsistent improvements, raising questions about the scope of benefits (Li et al., 2023). This gap is particularly problematic when organisations adopt chatbots with the expectation that better mental health will lead to higher engagement.

2.3 Workplace applications and adoption challenges

Within workplace settings, AI mental health chatbots are typically introduced as part of broader well-being programmes or digital benefits. Real-world programmes have demonstrated feasibility and uptake in large workforces, with chatbots used for triage and navigation to live support (Jackson-Triche et al., 2023). A systematic review of AI in workplace well-being identifies applications in stress monitoring, emotional support, and personalised interventions, but notes that many interventions are still at pilot or case-study stages (García-Madurga et al., 2024). Yuan et al. (2025) document more than 50 mental health-related chatbots, including 22 large language model-based tools, and argue that while these systems can provide accessible support, they often deliver generic content that does not fully address job-specific stressors such as workload pressures, role ambiguity, or interpersonal conflict.

Some workplace-focused studies offer promising but partial evidence. Banerjee et al. (2024) describe how chatbot-enhanced Mental Health First Aid can be integrated into corporate wellness frameworks, potentially reducing stigma and improving early detection. West et al. (2023) show that behavioural science-informed AI interventions can substantially increase employee assistance programme utilisation. However, these studies largely focus on engagement with mental health services (e.g., EAP access rates) rather than engagement with work tasks. The distinction is important: employees may become more engaged with support resources without necessarily becoming more energetic, dedicated, or absorbed in their jobs.

Ethical and practical challenges further complicate workplace adoption. Qualitative work on bots for mental health in organisations emphasises that human and technology agency interact—leadership, HR practices, and organisational context shape how chatbot insights are acted upon and thus how much they affect wellbeing (Dutta & Mishra, 2023). A scoping review of conversational AI in mental health care identifies privacy and confidentiality as the most frequently discussed concerns, followed by safety, harm, and the opacity of algorithmic decision-making (Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025). Grabb et al. (2024) evaluate several state-of-the-art language models in mental health emergency scenarios and conclude that many models may cause harm or provide inadequate responses. In Hong Kong, where job insecurity and long hours are common, fears that mental health data might be used against employees could strongly discourage authentic chatbot use (AXA, 2024). These issues suggest that even clinically effective chatbots may fail to deliver organisational benefits if employees do not feel safe using them.

2.4 From symptom reduction to job engagement

The literature reveals a disconnect between what is known about AI mental health chatbots and what organisations often hope to achieve. Clinical research demonstrates symptom reduction, and workplace pilots show improved engagement with services, but the pathway from chatbot usage to job engagement remains under-explored (Li et al., 2023; West et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2025).

Existing research only partially addresses the mechanisms that might link chatbot usage to engagement. Studies of AI adoption and mental health suggest that AI can both alleviate and exacerbate stress depending on implementation strategy and employee perceptions of control and fairness (Kim & Lee, 2024; Meduri et al., 2024). If AI tools are experienced as supportive, they may reduce distress and free up resources for work; if they are experienced as intrusive or unfair, they may increase stress and undermine trust. Very few studies, however, explicitly examine whether employees who use AI mental health chatbots report higher vigour, dedication, or absorption at work.

2.5 Literature synthesis: a JD–R-based mechanism

The following synthesis is grounded in the JD–R model, which posits that job demands can lead to strain and burnout, whereas job and personal resources foster engagement (Demerouti et al., 2001; Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). AI mental health chatbots are conceptualised as personal resources that provide accessible coping strategies, emotional support, and psychoeducation. The contribution of this synthesis is to translate clinical and usage-focused evidence into a workplace-relevant mechanism: if chatbot use reduces distress, employees may regain cognitive and emotional resources that support job engagement. Referencing PHQ-9 and GAD-7 also clarifies how “distress” can be monitored in practice when organisations evaluate mental health initiatives.

(access, frequency, features)

(UWES: vigour, dedication, absorption)
Potential moderators: usage frequency, perceived usefulness, organisational support (and context such as privacy concerns, trust, and local norms).

Theoretically informed expectations (for practice and future research).

One possibility is that employees who actively use AI mental health chatbots feel more supported and better equipped to cope with stress, leading directly to higher engagement. In JD–R terms, chatbots provide a personal resource that can buffer the effects of job demands on engagement (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017).

  • Expectation 1: AI mental health chatbot usage is positively associated with employee job engagement.

A more nuanced view is that chatbot usage influences engagement primarily through its impact on psychological distress. If employees use chatbots to process negative emotions, challenge unhelpful thoughts, or access timely support, they may experience reduced distress (Li et al., 2023). In turn, lower distress can free cognitive and emotional resources, allowing employees to invest more fully in their work and experience higher engagement (Schaufeli et al., 2006).

  • Expectation 2: Psychological distress mediates the relationship between AI mental health chatbot usage and job engagement.

Moderating factors.

  • Expectation 3: The relationship between chatbot usage and reduced psychological distress is stronger at higher levels of usage frequency.
  • Expectation 4: The relationship between chatbot usage and reduced psychological distress is stronger when perceived usefulness is high.
  • Expectation 5: The indirect effect of chatbot usage on job engagement via psychological distress is stronger when organisational support for mental health and AI tools is high.

3. Discussion and Managerial Implications

This report links AI mental health chatbot usage, psychological distress, and job engagement in one coherent pathway implied by the literature. While prior work often focuses on symptom reduction or service uptake, the JD–R synthesis above centres engagement with work itself and explains why reduced distress may translate into higher engagement when implementation conditions are favourable.

Managerial implications.

For managers and HR, the main implication is that procuring a chatbot is insufficient: adoption, trust, integration with human support, and credible privacy governance largely determine whether employees use tools authentically and whether symptom relief can plausibly free attention and energy for work. In Hong Kong workplaces, mental health is a sensitive topic and disclosure can be perceived as carrying career risk. Hong Kong-specific evidence reports substantial perceived discrimination against people with mental illness and concerns about workplace consequences (EOC, 2023). Separately, a widely cited general account of the stigma literature explains how stigma can discourage people from seeking help in the first place (Corrigan, 2004). Taken together, these points suggest that methods relying on open disclosure, such as interviews, focus groups, or open-ended reflective journals, may face lower participation and higher social desirability bias in routine organisational monitoring—an important design consideration when HR plans evaluation alongside chatbot rollouts.

Implementation quality still matters: benefits are more plausible when employees perceive chatbots as useful, feel safe using them, and see credible organisational support and privacy safeguards.

3.1 Limitations

First, the synthesis is literature-based and does not provide causal evidence. Second, chatbot effectiveness likely varies by design quality and integration with human support. Third, cultural explanations should be treated as plausible context-based mechanisms rather than definitive claims without empirical validation.

4. Recommendations

The following recommendations are directed at organisations considering or operating AI mental health chatbots as part of employee well-being programmes. They extend the literature synthesis and managerial discussion above.

  1. Monitor depression and anxiety severity with established self-administered tools. Consider PHQ-9 and GAD-7 as practical options to screen for and monitor severity over a two-week window. They can be completed privately, scored consistently, and interpreted using well-established cut points (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). This report does not conduct a survey; it recommends these measures as an option for evaluation and monitoring when implementing mental health initiatives, subject to ethics approval and professional oversight where required.
  2. Prioritise trust, privacy, and psychological safety. Communicate data handling clearly, limit unnecessary employer access to individual-level content where possible, and align chatbot use with crisis pathways and human escalation consistent with ethical guidance in the conversational-AI literature (Rahsepar Meadi et al., 2025; Grabb et al., 2024).
  3. Integrate chatbots with job-relevant stressors and live support. Avoid treating generic conversational support as a substitute for addressing workload, role clarity, and interpersonal conflict where those drive distress (Yuan et al., 2025; Jackson-Triche et al., 2023).
  4. Evaluate outcomes beyond uptake. Track not only registration or session counts but also whether employees report lower distress and, where feasible, whether work engagement indicators move in the expected direction (Schaufeli et al., 2006).
  5. Design for local context. In high power-distance, high-stigma settings such as many Hong Kong workplaces, emphasise low-visibility access, bilingual content where needed, and leadership modelling that normalises appropriate help-seeking without coercive monitoring (Mak et al., 2023; AXA, 2024).

5. Conclusion

This report adopted Approach 2: Literature Review & Recommendation. It critically evaluated evidence on AI mental health chatbots in clinical and workplace settings, synthesised a JD–R-based mechanism linking chatbot usage, psychological distress, and employee job engagement, and set out managerial implications for Hong Kong-style contexts where stigma and confidentiality concerns shape disclosure. Five theoretically informed expectations summarise how usage frequency, perceived usefulness, and organisational support may condition outcomes. The central gap—that existing research rarely examines job engagement directly, especially under high-pressure local conditions—remains important for both scholars and practitioners. For organisations seeking to monitor mental health initiatives responsibly, PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are recommended as established, self-administered tools for screening and monitoring depression and anxiety severity, alongside—not instead of—robust governance and human support pathways.
