1. Introduction

Research objectives and questions. Corresponding research questions include: how chatbot usage relates to employees’ vigour, dedication, and absorption at work; whether reduced psychological distress explains any observed relationship; and under what conditions chatbots are most likely to contribute to improved engagement.

3. Critical literature review

3.1 Clinical effectiveness of AI mental health chatbots

A growing body of research examines AI conversational agents for mental health support, including chatbots that deliver elements of cognitive behavioural therapy, mindfulness exercises, and general emotional support. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews have reported moderate improvements in depression and distress scores for users of such tools compared with controls or baseline measures insert 1–2 meta-analysis / systematic review citations. These findings demonstrate that AI chatbots can be clinically meaningful in reducing specific symptoms, particularly when integrated with mobile messaging and used consistently over time. However, some reviews also caution that improvements in broader well-being measures are less robust, raising questions about how symptom changes translate into functional outcomes such as work performance or engagement.

3.2 Workplace applications and adoption challenges

Within workplace contexts, AI mental health chatbots are often introduced as part of broader well-being programmes, employee assistance services, or digital health benefits. Studies have highlighted the potential for such interventions to increase employees’ engagement with mental health resources—for example, higher proportions of staff accessing support when AI-augmented outreach is used compared with traditional EAP promotion insert 1–2 workplace chatbot / AI wellbeing studies you use in 4418aprpdf. Yet, many of these studies focus on utilisation metrics (such as logins or message counts) rather than on whether employees become more energetic, dedicated, or absorbed in their work.

Researchers also note a mismatch between generic mental health chatbots and the specific stressors employees face at work, such as workload pressures, role ambiguity, or interpersonal conflict. Generic tools may offer useful coping strategies but may not fully address job-related drivers of distress. Moreover, concerns about data privacy, confidentiality, and surveillance are heightened in workplace settings, where employees may fear that sensitive information could affect their careers insert sources on AI ethics / privacy in workplaces. These concerns can dampen authentic usage and potentially reduce the effectiveness of chatbots as well-being tools.

3.3 From symptom reduction to job engagement

The existing literature suggests that AI chatbots can help reduce psychological symptoms and increase participation in mental health support services, but the pathway from these changes to job engagement remains under-explored. Some studies on AI adoption and employee burnout suggest that technology can both alleviate and exacerbate stress, depending on how it is implemented and supported within organisations insert 1–2 sources on AI, burnout, and job stress. However, very few studies explicitly test whether employees who use mental health chatbots subsequently report higher vigour, dedication, or absorption at work.

This gap has important implications for decision-makers. Organisations may be willing to invest in AI mental support chatbots if they can demonstrate not only symptom reduction but also improvements in attitudes and behaviours that are directly linked to performance. Without a clear theoretical and empirical understanding of how chatbot usage might translate into engagement, decisions remain partly speculative. The present report responds by integrating insights from the Job Demands–Resources model with evidence on AI mental health tools, and by proposing a mediational framework that can be tested in future empirical work.

4. Framework and relationship justification

The proposed framework is grounded in the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, which posits that job demands (such as workload or emotional strain) can lead to strain and burnout, whereas job and personal resources (such as social support, autonomy, or coping tools) foster engagement insert your JD-R source. AI mental support chatbots are conceptualised here as a form of personal resource that helps employees manage psychological demands by providing accessible coping strategies, emotional support, and psychoeducation.

Direct relationship: chatbot usage → job engagement. One possibility is that employees who actively use AI mental support chatbots feel more supported and better equipped to cope with stress, leading to higher energy, dedication, and absorption at work. For example, regular use of chatbot-guided breathing exercises or cognitive reframing techniques could help employees recover more quickly from daily hassles, enabling them to remain engaged during demanding periods insert source(s) on coping interventions and engagement if available. This leads to the first proposition that chatbot usage is positively associated with job engagement.

Mediated relationship: chatbot usage → psychological distress → job engagement. A more nuanced view, consistent with JD-R, is that chatbot usage primarily influences job engagement through its effect on psychological distress. Employees who use chatbots to process negative emotions, challenge unhelpful thoughts, or access timely support may experience reduced distress. In turn, lower distress can free cognitive and emotional resources, enabling employees to invest more fully in their work and to experience higher vigour, dedication, and absorption insert evidence that distress reduction is linked to engagement. This suggests that psychological distress mediates the relationship between chatbot usage and engagement.

Moderating factors. The strength of these relationships is likely to depend on how employees use chatbots and how organisations support their use. Higher usage frequency may be necessary to achieve meaningful changes in distress; perceived usefulness may influence whether employees engage deeply with the tool; and organisational support may determine whether staff feel safe to use chatbots for sensitive issues insert sources on technology acceptance / organisational support as moderators. In unsupportive environments, employees may fear monitoring or misuse of data, which could limit honest use and reduce potential benefits.

Together, these arguments lead to a set of testable propositions about the direct, indirect, and moderated relationships among chatbot usage, psychological distress, and job engagement. In a subsequent empirical study, these propositions could be translated into hypotheses and examined using survey data and structural equation modelling.

6. Discussion, implications, and limitations

The proposed framework contributes to the literature by explicitly connecting AI mental support chatbot usage with employee job engagement through the mediating role of psychological distress. For practitioners, it suggests that investments in AI mental health tools should not be evaluated solely on symptom scales or utilisation metrics, but also on whether employees feel more energetic, dedicated, and immersed in their work.

For organisations in Hong Kong, the framework highlights the importance of combining technological solutions with a supportive culture that encourages mental health help-seeking without stigma. Employers should communicate clearly about privacy protections, integrate chatbots with existing well-being initiatives, and provide training so that employees understand what the tools can and cannot do insert relevant HK or organisational practice sources. Without such organisational support, employees may under-use chatbots or use them superficially, limiting potential gains in both well-being and engagement.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the framework is conceptual and requires empirical testing; cross-sectional designs would limit conclusions about causality, and future research should consider longitudinal or experimental approaches. Second, self-report measures of distress, engagement, and chatbot usage are vulnerable to common method bias and social desirability effects. Third, the effectiveness of specific chatbots may vary widely depending on their design, content quality, and integration with human support, which means that generalising across tools should be done cautiously.

Future research could extend this framework by examining qualitative experiences of employees using chatbots at work, comparing AI-based support with human-delivered interventions, or exploring cross-cultural differences in how employees perceive and use AI for mental health. Such work would deepen understanding of when and how AI mental support chatbots can meaningfully enhance both well-being and engagement in modern organisations.
