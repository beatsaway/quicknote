The task

Learning English through Debating

It has recently been reported that some parents in Asian countries are installing apps on their children’s mobile phones that monitor their activities to ensure that they use these devices responsibly.

Write a letter to the Young Post stating whether you agree with these parents’ actions or not. Justify your position with three reasons.

Marking (CLO)

HKDSE Paper 2 Part B is assessed on Content (ideas, relevance, task fulfilment), Language (accuracy, range, tone/register) and Organisation (structure, cohesion, paragraphing). The levels below are indicative only for study — not official HKEAA marks.

Content — Level 4

  • Takes a clear stance (not fully agree) and gives three supporting reasons before a counterbalance — matches the task.
  • Ideas — online safety, healthy phone habits, parental peace of mind — are relevant to the stimulus.
  • The However paragraph adds maturity (trust, independence); keep it clearly linked to why full agreement is risky.
  • Could name one concrete app feature or region example briefly to push towards Level 5.

Language — Level 3

  • Meaning is usually clear, but grammar, articles and prepositions slip often (online/on the phone, relieve, plural agreement).
  • Some word choice is imprecise (relief, sex content, everyday); polish collocations for Level 4.
  • Register fits a youth-paper letter; opening salutation should match a formal letter to an editor.

Organisation — Level 4

  • Clear introduction, three reasons (First of all / Also / Furthermore), then However and conclusion.
  • Paragraphing matches stages of argument; the line of thought is easy to follow.
  • Watch repetition (“monitoring apps”) — occasional synonyms help cohesion at Level 5.

How to do better next time

  • Content: In the opening, name the newspaper and your position in one clean sentence so the examiner sees task engagement immediately.
  • Content: For three reasons, make each paragraph one main idea (safety / habits / trust or similar) with a sharp topic sentence.
  • Language: Build a proofread pass for prepositions (online, on the phone, in the real world) and word form (relieve / relief, sexual content).
  • Language: Read the conclusion aloud — remove duplicated words (should should) and tighten vague phrases (knowledge of using online → how to stay safe online).
  • Organisation: Signpost the counterargument (That said, … / On the other hand, …) so the move from pros to cons feels deliberate.

Overall (indicative): about Level 3–4 — strong ideas and structure; language accuracy limits the overall band.

Dear young post,

I am writing to talk about parents installing monitoring apps on their children's phones. I believe that these monitoring apps can be effective for children to use less phones. However, parents should be careful about using these apps. In my opinion, I do not fully agree with this action and I will explain my points in the following.

First of all, monitoring apps can help keep children safe in online. Nowadays, there are many dangers in online world like cyberbullying, sex content, and scams. If parents download a monitoring app in their children's phones, these situations can be prevented before they become serious as monitoring apps usually can block these content by their ai scanning in their phone. This can protect children from not only mental health but also their identity and money safe.

Also, these apps may help children learn how to use their phones wisely. Parents can set a time control in the app if their children spend too much time in their phone. For example, parents can set an everyday 2 hours game time and 3 hours social media time in their children's phone. This can remind them to focus on their study or spend more time outdoors. After they become adults, they are already trained to be able to control their electronic devices using time. This way, children can develop good habits and learn how to strike a balance between online world and real life.

Furthermore, monitoring apps can help parents relief their anxiety of knowing what they children doing. As children will grow older, they start to make their own choices, but parents may still worry about their safety. If parents can check their children's phone activities whenever they want, they will feel more relaxed and confident that their children are not in danger. This can help build connections between parents and children.

However, I think that parents should not rely too much on monitoring apps. It is important for children to learn how to be independent and be responsible on their own. Keep monitoring may make children feel not trusted which could damage the relationships between parents and children. Instead of relying on monitoring apps, parents should talk with their children and teach them about online safety which can solve the problem directly.

In conclusion, I believe that monitoring apps can be helpful if parents use it correctly. They can surely bring a lot of benefit for children and parents but it is also important for parents to trust their children and teach them about the knowledge of using online. Balance is the main thing to maintain both relationships. Parents should should use these tools wisely to support their children's growth and safety.

Yours sincerely,
Chris Wong

Dear Editor, I am writing to discuss whether parents should install monitoring apps on children’s phones. I do not fully support installing such apps without careful thought. Parents mean well, but apps alone cannot replace trust and conversation. I will give three reasons why these tools can help, then explain why I still stop short of full agreement. First and foremost, such apps can make the online world safer. Young users face cyber-bullying, scams and inappropriate material. Software that filters content and flags risky contacts can reduce harm before it escalates, protecting both wellbeing and personal data. Secondly, parental controls can encourage healthier habits. Time limits on games or social media nudge teenagers back toward homework, sleep and face-to-face time. If limits are discussed openly, children gradually learn to manage screen time themselves. Last but not least, transparent checks may ease parents’ anxiety when adolescents gain independence. Knowing there is a safety net can help families stay calmer during a stressful stage—provided parents explain why the app exists and respect privacy as far as reasonable. That said, surveillance should not replace dialogue. Over-monitoring can breed resentment and stunt the very responsibility parents want to build. Talking honestly about digital risks, modelling good behaviour and agreeing house rules often work better than silent tracking. In short, monitoring apps can be useful supports, not substitutes for parenting. Balance, consent and education matter as much as the technology itself. Yours sincerely, Chris Wong
