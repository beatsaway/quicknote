Task

Commentary videos: concerns and platforms

Write a short piece for your school newsletter or letters page.

Commentary videos are becoming more popular on YouTube, but not without concerns:

  • “Content farming”: reusing other people’s clips with very little new material for the audience.
  • Not always based on facts: some exaggerate or misread the original on purpose to get more views.

How serious do you think these concerns are, and why? Suggest two improvements YouTube could make (e.g. reporting rules, censoring, demonetising, bans, etc.).

Useful words content farming(內容農場式片), rehash(翻炒), misleading(誤導), exaggerate(誇大), clickbait(標題黨), demonetise(取消廣告分成), platform policy(平台政策), copyright(版權), community guidelines(社群守則), misinformation(假資訊)

What went well

You picked up both parts of the brief—lazy reuse and misleading tone—not just one.

  • 🎯 Topic match — you name copy-and-paste style videos and warn readers not to trust everything.
  • ⚖️ Ethics — you say stretching or mocking the original can disrespect creators; that answers “how serious?” from a human angle.
  • 🛡️ Action — you mention reporting and looking for “fake” information; that ties the piece to what viewers can do.
  • 📰 Newsletter feel — direct address (“you”) and short sentences suit a letters page.

Could be even better

  • 💡 Task requirement: spell out two concrete YouTube changes (e.g. clearer reuse / reupload rules, stricter demonetisation for misleading titles, faster review when many users report the same issue). One sentence each is enough.
  • 💡 Seriousness: add one line on why it matters—e.g. wasted time, unfair to small creators, or false stories spreading in group chats.
  • 💡 Word choice: “translation” here likely means a reupload or rehash; “translation” usually means turning text into another language.
  • 💡 Grammar: “there is something” (not “are”); want to succeed or want success; “videos which give” (plural).
  • 💡 Brand: capitals for YouTube; avoid telling readers “read this paragraph” in a published letter—use Secondly, or Another problem is.

Comments

Your instinct is right: commentary is not automatically trustworthy—some channels add analysis, but others barely edit someone else’s footage or twist it for laughs and clicks. Calling that out in a school newsletter is useful because many students discover news and drama through YouTube first.

The task also asks for platform-level ideas. Reporting alone puts the burden on viewers; policies such as stricter limits on reused footage without commentary, or down-ranking proven misinformation, are the kind of “two improvements” examiners like to see named clearly. The yellow highlights keep your ideas; they mainly tighten English and newsletter tone.
