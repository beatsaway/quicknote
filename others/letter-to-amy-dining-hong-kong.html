Dear Amy,

It's a long time ago when I have sent an e-mail. I have already seen the e-mail you have sent last week. I will tell you more about the three etiquettes if it is necessary to do it or not.

I made a table for you to understand the etiquettes better:

  1. You shouldn’t stick chopsticks into a bowl of rice as it represents giving food to the dead and a burner for worshipping them. It is filled with unluckiness in Chinese traditions.
  2. You should use a pair of serving chopsticks when you want to take some food. This etiquette makes people think you are clean and tidy. However, if you just use your own one, others will think that you are unpolite.
  3. Filial piety is the most important thing in Chinese tradition, so you should let your parents (or grandparents, if necessary) to move their chopsticks.

Don't worry about them. Just follow them, then it's ok.

Demo version

Dear Amy,

It's been a while since I last wrote, so I was glad to read your email last week. Don't be apprehensive about dining here. Below is my honest advice on the three tips you found online.

1. Upright chopsticks in rice. Yes, follow this in Hong Kong. At family meals and yum cha, sticking chopsticks upright in a bowl is a taboo because it looks symbolic of funeral incense. Do lay chopsticks flat on the rest or on the holder.

2. Serving chopsticks. Yes, in communal dining. When dishes are shared, it is customary to use serving chopsticks or a clean spoon. In a very informal setting with close friends, people may relax the rule, but using serving utensils still shows good manners.

3. Wait for the eldest or host. Yes, especially at home or when your host is the guest of honour. Wait until parents, grandparents, or the host pick up their chopsticks before you start. At a quick school lunch, the rule is softer, but watching others first is still polite.

Hong Kong hospitality is warm. Locals will put you at ease if you show respect and adaptability. Ask quietly if you are unsure, and enjoy the food.

Best wishes,
[Your name]
